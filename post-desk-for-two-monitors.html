The short verdict: buy 160 × 80 cm (63 × 31.5 in) if you have the wall for it, and 140 × 70 cm (55 × 27.5 in) if you don't. Width gets the monitors onto the desk; depth is what stops them sitting 30cm from your face. Given a choice between wider and deeper for two screens, take deeper — and put both panels on a dual arm so the stands stop eating your surface.

The numbers that decide whether it fits

Almost every "my dual setup feels cramped" complaint traces back to a product photo instead of a tape measure. A 24-inch panel is about 54cm wide and a 27-inch about 61cm — glass and bezel only, before the stand and before any gap between the screens. Two 27-inch panels side by side occupy 122–130cm of clear surface on their own.

Those are the sizes the market actually sells: 120, 140, 160 and 180cm across the UK and Europe, 48 to 70 inches in the US. Japan, Hong Kong and Singapore skew smaller — 100–120cm tops at 50–60cm depth are normal in apartments, exactly where a dual arm stops being an accessory and becomes essential.

Why depth matters more than width

Comfortable viewing distance is roughly an arm's length: 50–70cm from your eyes to the glass. On a 60cm-deep desk the keyboard pushes the panels to the rear edge — and the rear edge still isn't far enough away, so you either lean in or let the screens overhang.

70cm is the practical floor for two screens. 80cm is where the setup stops feeling like a compromise, because both panels can sit back at a healthy distance with room left for a keyboard and a notebook. If your room only allows 60cm of depth, buy a monitor arm before a bigger desk: it recovers 20–25cm per screen.

Set the screens up once

  • Primary screen dead centre, top third level with your eyes; the second screen is reference, angled 15–30° inward. Centring the bezel gap avoids a day of neck-turning.
  • Seat height sets elbow angle, not desk height. Sitting down and measuring seated elbow height before buying a fixed desk is worth more than any spec table.
  • One cable path. Two DisplayPort or USB-C runs plus power — route them before you mount anything. A tray under the rear edge does more for how tidy the setup feels than anything on top of it.

If the panels are mismatched, matching resolution matters more than matching model — 4K beside 1080p gives text-size differences scaling never fully fixes.

What to skip

  • Wide, 60cm-deep gaming desks. Width you can't use and depth you need.
  • Glass tops. Clamps need a solid core; glass either cracks or forces you back onto stands.
  • Corner desks with a fixed monitor shelf. The shelf sits too high once you add an arm.
  • Single-motor frames at 160cm. More width means more leverage, and uneven lift is what people feel as wobble.

Frequently asked

How wide does a desk need to be for two monitors?

Two 24-inch panels: 120cm minimum, 140cm comfortable. Two 27-inch panels: 140cm minimum, 160cm comfortable. A 27-inch panel is about 61cm wide, so the pair already uses 122–130cm before bezels and speakers.

Is 60cm depth enough for two monitors?

It functions, but it's the wrong compromise. Ideal eye-to-screen distance is 50–70cm; on a 60cm desk the keyboard pushes the panels closer than that. 70cm is the practical floor, 80cm is comfortable.

Stands or a dual monitor arm?

Arms, for a dual setup. They remove 20–25cm of stand depth per screen and let each panel sit independently. Check the per-arm weight rating and that your desktop has a solid core for the clamp.

Will a standing desk hold two monitors?

Easily — most dual-motor frames are rated 60–100kg against a load of perhaps 15kg. The real question is stability when raised: prefer 3-stage legs or a crossbar if two 27-inch screens ride on a dual arm.

Can a dual-monitor setup fit in a small flat?

Yes — prioritise depth over width. A 140 × 70 desk beats a 160 × 60 one, and on a 120cm desk a dual arm plus inward angling recovers the width the stands would have taken.
